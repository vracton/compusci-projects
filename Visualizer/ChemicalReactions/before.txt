Current iodine clock simulation method

1. Inputs and initialization

The simulation starts from stock solutions defined in ChemicalDriver.cs. Each solution has:
- volume in mL
- concentration in g/L
- solute molar mass
- which simulated species it contributes

The code converts g/L to molarity as:

    concentration_M = grams_per_liter / molar_mass_g_per_mol

Then it computes the initial mixed bulk concentrations by summing contributed moles and dividing by total volume.

Particles are also seeded into the box in layers based on the starting solutions, but the particles are now mainly for visualization. They do not drive the chemistry timing anymore.


2. General simulation flow

Each update step does this:

- advance the displayed particles in the box
- advance the bulk chemistry using concentration-based substeps
- convert the resulting bulk concentrations back into target particle counts
- add/remove particles so the visualization tracks the bulk state
- update the bulk hue and record first orange / first blue timestamps
- write a CSV row for diagnostics


3. Current chemical substep order

The chemistry is run in a fixed sequence each substep:

1. Slow iodate + bisulfite reaction
   IO3^- + HSO3^- derived step producing I^-, SO4^2-, and H^+

2. Mercury sink
   Excess iodide is scavenged by Hg^2+ into HgI2.
   This is handled as a fast physical sink using HgI2 solubility rather than an ordinary collision event.

3. Iodine formation
   Remaining iodide reacts with iodate and acid to form iodine.

4. Iodine quench by bisulfite
   Residual bisulfite destroys iodine.

5. Starch binding
   Surviving iodine binds starch and becomes the blue starch complex proxy.


4. How slow and fast behavior is represented

Different process speeds are represented in two ways:

A. Reaction rate constants

Each finite-rate process has its own rate constant. The reaction extent for a substep is:

    extent = rate * step_time

where the rate is built from the rate constant times concentration terms.

For example:
- iodate/bisulfite feed step uses IodateBisulfiteRate
- iodine-forming step uses IodineFormationRate
- iodine quench uses IodineBisulfiteQuenchRate
- starch binding uses StarchBindingRate

Larger rate constants make the process happen faster in a given chemical state.

B. Fast physical sink / equilibrium handling

The HgI2 precipitation step is not treated as a normal slow kinetic step. It is treated as a fast sink that immediately removes excess free iodide until the free iodide level is consistent with the HgI2 solubility-product rule currently being used.


5. Why the substep ordering matters

The processes are not independent. Earlier steps starve later steps of reactants.

- If the iodate/bisulfite step is slow, the whole clock is slow.
- If the mercury sink is very effective, iodide is removed before it can feed iodine formation.
- If residual bisulfite is still high, newly formed iodine is quenched before blue can build up.
- Only iodine that survives the sink/quench competition can create the blue starch complex.

Because of this, timing depends on both the rate constants and the order in which the chemistry is applied.


6. Current definition of visible color

Orange is currently tied to HgI2 exceeding a visible-concentration threshold.
Blue is currently tied to the starch-complex proxy exceeding a visible-concentration threshold, plus a residual-bisulfite cutoff.

The bulk hue is then reported as:
- clear
- orange
- blue

based on those bulk state thresholds, not based on the first colored particle.


7. Potential reasons the model becomes inaccurate for other recipes

The current model was tuned to one recipe and can be inaccurate away from that point for several reasons:

- The rate laws are approximate rather than experimentally validated for the full Old Nassau system.
- The visibility thresholds are fixed and may not scale correctly with other concentrations or total volumes.
- HgI2 precipitation is simplified to a solubility-based sink and omits real precipitation dynamics.
- The iodine/starch chemistry is simplified; real blue color behavior involves richer iodine/polyiodide/starch behavior.
- Important intermediates and equilibria are missing, such as triiodide or additional iodine speciation.
- Acid dependence is simplified; the iodate/iodide branch is strongly pH-sensitive.
- The model is bulk and does not fully simulate real stirring or fluid mixing effects.
- The same tuned parameter set may only work well near the concentration regime it was fitted on.
- Particle visualization is a discretized proxy for the bulk state and can distort apparent behavior at low or high concentrations.
- Errors in stock solution assumptions or reagent form can shift the true initial conditions.
- The system contains both very fast and slower processes, so a simple explicit substep approach may not stay accurate across all recipes.


8. Practical interpretation

Right now the simulation should be viewed as a tuned phenomenological bulk model with particle visualization, not a universally predictive first-principles chemistry simulator.

It can be made to match a target recipe reasonably well, but accuracy across many different recipes will likely require:
- better validated kinetics
- more complete chemistry and equilibria
- better optical visibility modeling
- or fitting against multiple experimental recipes rather than one
